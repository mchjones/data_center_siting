Balancing environmental impact with operational costs in data center siting

This repository contains the data and code used for this project.

In response to the rapid proliferation of AI, the past few years have seen an explosion in the planning and construction of data centers [LBNL 2024 Report, IEA Energy and AI report]. System operators of the electrical grid, subject to minimal demand growth over the past decade, are struggling to respond to this sudden increase in demand: interconnection queues are overloaded, IRPs are listing hundreds of gigawatts of needed investments, and capacity markets are hitting record-high prices [Verse, Georgia Power IRP, EIA Capex/Opex, EIA article on new load growth, PJM]. Water systems are similarly stressed, with increasing droughts across the country due to climate change and a need for more complex management practices exacerbating the impacts of this new substantial consumer class [NOAA,USGS].

Where these facilities are constructed will have substantial implications for investment needs and environmental impacts. The generation mix of the grid at the point of interconnection for these data centers will determine the data-center-driven water consumption and carbon emissions [Siddik 2021]. Already, the natural-gas-heavy response to data center demand is raising alarm about the emissions impact of the data center buildout [Latitude, Vries-Gao]. Research has identified regions where the water consumption and carbon emissions of potential data centers are minimized; however, there is no indication that data centers are purposefully being constructed in these regions [Siddik 2021, Harvard Working Paper]. AI companies are primarily concerned with the cost of electricity, tax breaks, speed to power, and fiber connectivity in their siting decisions – not environmental impact [Intel, Washington Times].

In response to this tension between private interest and public good, we aim to characterize the tradeoffs between operational costs and environmental impact of data centers in the United States. To do this, for a model data center and pairwise combinations of locations and cooling system configurations, we will calculate (1) the water and energy costs, as well as the taxes, associated with data center operation and (2) carbon emissions and water consumption. These results will be used to identify region and cooling system combinations that both minimize operational cost and environmental impacts, thus constituting optimal siting locations that balance private interest and public good. We will also identify regions that perform well on one metric and poorly on the other, thus good candidates for incentives or regulation.
